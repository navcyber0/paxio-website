Helping your kid tell real from fake online — a practical starting point

Paxio·September 16, 2026·~4 min read
Photo by John on Unsplash

"Teach your kid media literacy" is hard to act on, it sounds like a school subject, not something you can practice at the dinner table. The useful version is narrower: a few specific habits, applied to whatever a kid's actually looking at that day.

This now includes AI, not just "fake news"

About one-in-five US teens say they use AI chatbots to get news, per Pew Research Center's February 2026 report on how teens use and view AI, and roughly one-in-ten teens who view AI negatively specifically cite it as "a threat or ripe for misuse." Media literacy in 2026 has to cover AI-sourced information and AI-generated content, not just the older "check the source" advice built for a pre-AI internet. (Pew Research Center, "How Teens Use and View AI," February 24, 2026)

The algorithm isn't neutral, worth saying explicitly

A feed isn't a representative sample of what's out there, it's optimized to keep a kid watching, which favors content that provokes reaction over content that's simply accurate. Not a conspiracy framing, just how engagement-ranking works. "This feed shows me what keeps me watching, not what's true" is a real, usable filter, more usable than "be skeptical."

The habit that matters most: check before you share

Not "don't believe everything you see", a concrete pause before resharing. Misinformation spreads mostly through sharing, not the original post reaching people directly, so the moment that matters is "should I pass this along," not "should I believe this." Checking who posted it and whether it's been reported elsewhere catches most of what matters, without requiring full-time fact-checking.

AI-generated content needs naming as its own thing

Photos and video that look real but are AI-generated or significantly altered are common enough now that "it looks real" isn't evidence it is. Worth naming specifically, not folding into a general "some stuff is fake" talk, a kid taught to spot old-style photoshop tells won't necessarily catch this.

What works less well: correcting individual claims

"That's not true, here's the real story" only covers that one claim, the next piece of misinformation needs the same correction from scratch. Teaching the checking habit generalizes; correcting claims doesn't.

Three questions to practise at the dinner table

Habits form through repetition, and the easiest place to repeat them is in ordinary conversation. Pick something your child has seen this week, such as a viral clip, a surprising claim or an ad, and ask three things:

  • Who made this, and why? A person, a company, a bot? Are they trying to inform, entertain or sell?
  • How do we know it's true? Can we find the same thing from a source that has nothing to gain?
  • What does it want me to do? Share, buy, click, get angry? Content that pushes a strong reaction quickly deserves a slower look.

Keep it light and curious, and admit when you're not sure either. Children learn far more from watching an adult check something than from being told to.

Can content filtering help with misinformation?

Content filtering and screen time manage exposure, which sites are reachable, how much time on feed-based apps, not the truthfulness of what's inside an allowed app. Genuinely outside what device-level controls can do.

Content filtering, app blocking, and screen time limits handle the exposure side in the background, so the literacy habits above still happen through conversation, not configuration.

Frequently asked questions

How do I teach my child to spot fake information online?

Teach one habit first: check before you share. Ask who made it, whether other sources say the same, and whether it is trying to make you feel something fast.

At what age should kids learn digital literacy?

Start as soon as they use screens independently. Keep it simple and concrete for younger children and add more nuance about algorithms and AI-generated content as they grow.

Can content filtering protect kids from misinformation?

No. Filtering blocks categories of sites, not the truthfulness of what is inside an allowed site or app. Literacy has to come from conversation.
