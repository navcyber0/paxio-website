Cyberbullying: what it actually looks like, and how to notice it

Paxio·August 31, 2026·~3 min read
Photo by Luiz Rogério Nunes on Unsplash

"Cyberbullying" gets talked about as one big scary category, which makes it hard to know what to actually watch for. In practice, it's rarely a single dramatic incident — it's a pattern, and each piece of that pattern looks small on its own.

It's more common than most parents assume

About three-in-ten teen Snapchat users, and roughly one-in-five on TikTok and Instagram, have experienced at least one of three harassment types — offensive name-calling, rumors spread about them, or physical threats — according to Pew Research Center's April 2026 report on teens' experiences across the three platforms. Across all three, about three-quarters of teens see harassment and bullying as a real problem for people their age, even if they haven't personally experienced it. This isn't a rare-edge-case problem — it's a routine part of being a teenager on these platforms. (Pew Research Center, "Teens' Experiences on TikTok, Instagram and Snapchat," April 15, 2026)

The defining feature: repeated, not a single message

What separates cyberbullying from an unpleasant one-off comment is repetition, targeting, and a power imbalance the target usually can't escape, since it follows them onto their own phone. That distinction matters for response: a single incident might call for a conversation; a pattern calls for real intervention.

What it actually looks like

  • Exclusion — deliberately left out of a group chat, sometimes one created specifically to exclude them
  • Impersonation — a fake account, or their real account used without permission
  • Public pile-ons — a comment thread where several people join in, escalating faster than one-on-one conflict
  • Private content shared publicly — screenshots or messages shared outside their original context, to embarrass someone
  • Message volume as a tactic — overwhelming rather than communicative

None of these look alarming in isolation from the outside.

What actually tips a parent off

Not the content of any one message — most parents shouldn't be reading every private chat. What tends to surface it is a real, sustained change: avoiding a device they used to be attached to, unusual anxiety at a notification sound, withdrawing from a friend group with no explanation, mood tracking with phone use more than anything else. No single sign is proof, but a cluster is worth a direct, low-pressure conversation.

What actually helps

The instinct to lock everything down after learning about bullying is understandable — but kids who believe disclosure means losing their phone are less likely to disclose in the first place. Making that consequence explicitly not the case removes the biggest barrier to a kid asking for help. Beyond that: knowing how to block/report on the platform involved, saving screenshots before blocking, and looping in the school if classmates are involved.
