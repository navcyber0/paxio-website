AI chatbots and kids: what's actually risky, and what isn't

Paxio·August 26, 2026·~4 min read
Photo by Andrew Guan on Unsplash

The homework-cheating angle is the one most parents think to worry about. It's real, but it's not where the actual risk sits. The bigger shift is that a lot of kids are using AI chatbots as something closer to a conversation partner — and most parents don't know it's happening.

The scale of this is bigger than "occasional homework help"

Common Sense Media's 2026 Census on AI use by kids and teens — surveying over a thousand 9- to 17-year-olds — found that 86% of kids that age use or interact with AI in some form, rising to 92% among 16- to 17-year-olds. The gap that matters most for a parent: only 37% of parents know their teen is using AI at all. Nearly half of kids surveyed said they'd never had a conversation with a parent about AI safety. (Common Sense Media, "Census: AI Use by Tweens and Teens," June 8, 2026)

That gap — kids using it constantly, parents mostly unaware — is the actual starting problem, more than any specific chatbot feature.

What "using AI" actually means for most kids

Not just asking for help with an essay. A separate Common Sense Media study found most teens have used AI companions — chatbots designed for conversation, not just Q&A — and about a third said they've discussed something serious with one instead of talking to a real person. Some described AI conversations as more satisfying than talking to actual friends. (Common Sense Media, "Talk, Trust, and Trade-Offs," 2025)

This isn't kids sneaking around a homework rule. It's kids treating a chatbot as somewhere to bring things they're not bringing to a person.

Why that specifically is the risk

A companion chatbot doesn't get tired, judge, or say the wrong thing at the wrong moment — which is exactly what makes it appealing and exactly what makes it a bad substitute for a real conversation. Common Sense Media's mental-health-focused risk assessment, done with Stanford Medicine's Brainstorm Lab, found that major AI platforms consistently fail to recognize signs of real distress the way a person would, and that safety in a conversation tends to degrade the longer it goes — meaning a late-night conversation that starts casually can drift somewhere worse without the same guardrails holding. (Common Sense Media, November 2025)

None of that means every AI conversation is a crisis. It means the risk isn't "the AI gives a wrong homework answer" — it's a kid substituting a chatbot for a person during something that actually needed a person.

What's less risky than it sounds

Using AI to summarize an article, brainstorm, or get unstuck on a problem isn't the same category as the above — it's closer to using a search engine or a calculator, and treating it with the same suspicion as a companion chatbot mostly just teaches a kid to stop mentioning it. Worth distinguishing out loud: "asking AI for homework help" and "talking to AI about how you're feeling" are different things worth different levels of attention.

How can parents handle AI chatbots?

Not a ban, and not surveillance of every message. The awareness gap above is the fixable part — most parents don't know this is happening at all, so the AI conversation is never happening at home either. Asking directly, without alarm ("do you ever talk to an AI chatbot, what's that like") does more than any device setting, and it only works if it happens before a kid feels like they'd be in trouble for saying yes.

Can a parental control app block AI chatbots?

Content filtering and screen time control which apps are reachable and for how long — they don't see what's inside a conversation in an allowed app, and they're not built to.

Frequently asked questions

Are AI chatbots safe for kids?

It depends on how they are used. Homework help is very different from long, emotional conversations with a companion-style bot, and the second is where most of the concern sits.

How do I know if my child uses AI chatbots?

Ask directly and without judgment, and check which AI apps are installed. Device-level tools can limit or block specific AI apps, but they can't see what is said inside them.

Can a parental control app block AI chatbot apps?

Yes, an app blocker can switch off a specific AI app on your child's Android phone. It cannot filter or read the conversations inside an app that is allowed.
