Sextortion and kids: what parents should know, and what to do if it happens

By Naveenkumar Arulmani, Founder of Paxio·October 25, 2026·~4 min read

Sextortion is when someone threatens to share a child’s private images unless the child sends more images, does something else, or pays money. If it happens to your child: stay calm, tell them they are not in trouble, do not pay, block the account but keep the messages and profile as evidence, and report it. That is the advice of the US National Center for Missing & Exploited Children (NCMEC).

This is a difficult subject, so we keep it practical. We are not lawyers or counsellors, and this is general information. If your child is in immediate danger or talking about hurting themselves, call 112 now.

What is sextortion, and who is targeted?

NCMEC describes sextortion as a form of child sexual exploitation in which a child is threatened or blackmailed, often with the threat of sharing nude or sexual images, to get more sexual content, sexual activity or money. Offenders are often people the child met online, and they may use stolen images or fake accounts. NCMEC reports a sharp rise in financial sextortion, where money is demanded, and says teenage boys have been the most common targets in recent cases.

What are the warning signs of a sextortion attempt?

NCMEC lists red flags on the offender’s side: someone who quickly asks for nude images after becoming friends, who uses several false identities, or who moves the conversation to a private app. Treat any of these as a stop sign, even if the person seems friendly.

In your child you may notice sudden worry about their phone, secrecy, trouble sleeping or distress after messages arrive. These signs also fit many other problems, so ask gently rather than assume.

What should you do if your child is being blackmailed?

  1. Stay calm and reassure your child. The blackmailer is to blame, not your child. Say it out loud.
  2. Do not pay or comply. NCMEC says cooperating or paying rarely stops the blackmail, and advises getting help before deciding anything.
  3. Stop replying and block the account, but do not delete anything. Keep the profile and messages, because they can help stop the blackmailer. Take screenshots and note usernames and links.
  4. Report it on the app using its safety or report feature.
  5. Report to the police. Tell your local police, and use the official online reporting channels for cyber crime. Check the current process on the official sites, because it can change.
  6. Ask for the images to be taken down. Services such as NCMEC’s Take It Down can help remove explicit images of someone under 18 from participating platforms. Check the service’s own page for what it covers.
  7. Look after your child. Shame is the real danger here. If they seem very low or talk about self-harm, call 112 and speak to a doctor or counsellor.

How do you talk about this before it ever happens?

Children who know what to expect are harder to trap. A short, calm talk is enough:

  • Anyone who asks for private pictures, even someone who seems like a friend, is a red flag.
  • Nobody can force you to send more because you sent one.
  • Never move a chat to a private app because a stranger asks you to.
  • If anything like this happens, tell me. You will not be in trouble, and I will help.

That last promise matters most. Kids stay silent because they are afraid of punishment or of losing the phone. Say clearly that you will help first and talk about rules later.

What phone settings reduce the risk?

Settings cannot replace the conversation, but they cut exposure: set social accounts to private, limit who can message your child, and remove chat features in games and apps your child does not need. Frequently asked questions

Does paying the blackmailer make it stop?

NCMEC says cooperating or paying rarely stops the blackmail. Stop contact, keep the evidence and report it.

Can explicit images of my child be removed from the internet?

Services such as NCMEC’s Take It Down can help remove explicit images of someone under 18 from participating platforms. Coverage depends on which platforms take part, so also report on the app and to the police.

Is it my child’s fault if they sent a picture?

No. The blackmailer is responsible. Children are often tricked by fake accounts or pressured step by step, so the priority is to support them and stop the blackmail.
