How to block apps on your child's phone (and why the built-in way often isn't enough)

Paxio·September 17, 2026·~6 min read
Photo by John Onaeko on Unsplash

"Just block the app" sounds like a single setting. In practice, there are three different ways to do it, and they hold up very differently once a kid actually wants back in.

Uninstalling isn't blocking

Deleting an app stops it today. It does nothing about tomorrow, when the same app gets reinstalled from the app store in under a minute — deletion isn't a control, it's a delay, and a short one if app-install approval isn't also locked down separately.

Android's built-in app pausing is real, but easy to undo

Android (via Digital Wellbeing or a device's own "Focus Mode") can pause specific apps for a set time. It works, but it typically lives inside a menu the child can also reach, with no separate PIN protecting it — a control that lives on the same device, behind the same lock screen, as the person it's meant to restrict is a control that mostly works on cooperation, not enforcement.

What makes an app block actually work?

Three things make the difference between a setting and a real block:

  • Enforced in real time, not just scheduled. A block that only applies during set hours still leaves the app open the rest of the day.
  • Behind a separate parent PIN, not the device's own unlock — otherwise "blocked" just means "one settings menu away from unblocked."
  • Survives a reinstall. If deleting and reinstalling the app resets its status to allowed, the block was never really a block.

Blocking by app vs. blocking by category

Per-app blocking (turn off Instagram specifically) and content filtering by category (turn off all social media, or all gambling sites) solve different problems. App blocking is precise — good when the concern is one specific app. Category filtering is broader — good when the concern is "anything like this," not one name. Most families end up using both: category filtering as the baseline, specific app blocks layered on top for the one or two apps that need it by name.

Which apps are worth blocking first?

Most families don't need to block much. A short, deliberate list beats a long one your child will resent. A useful test is to block by what an app lets people do, not by how popular it is:

  • Apps with open chat or messaging from strangers. Anonymous chat apps, random video-chat apps and live-streaming apps where anyone can message your child are the highest-risk category.
  • Apps rated above your child's age. Age ratings on the Play Store aren't perfect, but an app rated for teens or adults is a clear signal to wait.
  • Apps built around spending. Games with loot boxes, gacha mechanics or constant purchase prompts are designed to make spending feel unreal to a child.
  • Apps you can't explain. If you couldn't tell a friend in one sentence what the app is for, look it up before it gets a place on your child's phone.

Block, limit or allow? Choosing the right setting

Blocking isn't the only lever, and it isn't always the right one. A simple way to decide:

  • Block apps where the risk is the app itself, such as stranger chat or adult-rated content.
  • Limit apps that are fine in small doses, such as a favourite game, so it can be capped at, say, 30 minutes without touching the overall daily budget.
  • Allow everything your child genuinely needs for school, family contact and everyday life. Over-blocking pushes kids to look for workarounds.

It also helps to decide, ahead of time, what happens when your child asks to unblock something. A rule such as "ask me, and we'll look at it together" turns blocking into a conversation instead of a standoff.

What to do when your child asks to unblock something

It will happen, and it's a good sign: a child who asks is a child who isn't quietly working around you. Listen for the actual reason (a friend is playing it, a school project needs it) and check the app together. If it's fine, unblock it and say why. If it isn't, explain the specific concern rather than just saying no. Revisit blocked apps every few months, because what was unsuitable at nine may be reasonable at twelve.

Frequently asked questions

How do I block an app on my child's phone?

Use a parental control app with per-app blocking, protected by a parent PIN, so a blocked app stays blocked rather than being one settings menu away from unblocked.

Is uninstalling an app the same as blocking it?

No. An uninstalled app can be reinstalled from the app store in a minute unless installs are also restricted.

Can I block apps only at certain times?

Yes, combine per-app blocking with a bedtime schedule or daily screen time limit, depending on whether the concern is one app or overall time.
