Fake app clones: how a copycat gets millions of downloads before anyone notices

Paxio·Draft·~2 min read
Photo by William Hook on Unsplash

A kid looking for a new game or a "free skin pack" app doesn't check who published it — they check whether the icon looks right and whether it has a decent star rating. That's a reasonable way to judge an app. It's also exactly what a convincing clone is built to pass. This isn't a kid-judgment problem; it's a mobile-specific one, because an app store is the one place software gets judged almost entirely on how it looks in a small grid of icons before anyone opens it.

How big this actually gets before it's caught

In May 2026, security researchers at ESET uncovered a campaign they named "CallPhantom" — 28 Android apps on Google Play that claimed to show anyone's call history, texts, and WhatsApp logs for a fee. The data was fake, generated by the app itself. Google removed all 28 apps, but not before they'd been downloaded more than 7.3 million times combined, with one single app responsible for over 3 million of those. (ESET WeLiveSecurity, "Fake call logs, real payments: How CallPhantom tricks Android users," May 2026)

Worth sitting with: these weren't sideloaded apps from a sketchy website. They sat in the official Play Store, passing Google's own review process, long enough to rack up millions of installs. If official app store review can miss it for months, a kid skimming search results for a free game mod was never the actual failure point.

Why cloning works so well on a phone

A phone screen shows an icon, a name, and a star rating — not much else before you tap "Install." There's no address bar to inspect, no easy way to see who actually built it, and an icon at thumbnail size is trivial to copy pixel-for-pixel. The same trick works just as well for a fake "Minecraft skin pack" or a fake "Roblox executor" as it does for a fake call-log app — same mechanism, different bait, and the bait that reaches kids is almost always framed around a free game or in-app currency.

What actually helps

  • Check the publisher name, not just the icon: tap through to the developer's name on the listing. A copycat's name is usually slightly off, with no other apps published under it.
  • Read the most recent reviews, not the average rating: a fake app can rack up early positive reviews before word gets out; the last few weeks are more honest than the overall star count.
  • Be skeptical of anything promising free premium currency, unlocks, or "hacks": the exact bait behind the Minecraft and Roblox clone campaigns, and a stronger warning sign than anything about how the app looks.
  • Weigh install count against review count: millions of downloads with almost no reviews, or reviews that all sound alike, is a pattern worth pausing on.

Where Paxio fits, and where it doesn't

App blocking works after the fact — once a fake app is identified, it can be blocked from running on your child's device. It can't retroactively vet an app your kid is about to install for the first time; there's no database that flags a brand-new clone the moment it appears. The publisher-name and recent-reviews check above is the step that happens before anything reaches the device at all.

Paxio's App Control lets you see what's actually installed on your child's device and block anything you don't recognize, which matters most for exactly this kind of app — the one that looked legitimate enough to install in the first place.
