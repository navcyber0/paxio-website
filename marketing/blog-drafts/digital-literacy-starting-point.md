# Helping your kid tell real from fake online — a practical starting point

_Meta description: "Media literacy" sounds like a school subject, but the actual skill kids need is a lot more specific than that. Here's a concrete starting point, not an abstract lecture._

_~2 min read_

"Teach your kid media literacy" is hard to act on — it sounds like a school subject, not something you can practice at the dinner table. The useful version is narrower: a few specific habits, applied to whatever a kid's actually looking at that day.

## This now includes AI, not just "fake news"

About one-in-five US teens say they use AI chatbots to get news, per Pew Research Center's February 2026 report on how teens use and view AI — and roughly one-in-ten teens who view AI negatively specifically cite it as "a threat or ripe for misuse." Media literacy in 2026 has to cover AI-sourced information and AI-generated content, not just the older "check the source" advice built for a pre-AI internet. ([Pew Research Center, "How Teens Use and View AI," February 24, 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/))

## The algorithm isn't neutral — worth saying explicitly

A feed isn't a representative sample of what's out there — it's optimized to keep a kid watching, which favors content that provokes reaction over content that's simply accurate. Not a conspiracy framing, just how engagement-ranking works. "This feed shows me what keeps me watching, not what's true" is a real, usable filter — more usable than "be skeptical."

## The habit that matters most: check before you share

Not "don't believe everything you see" — a concrete pause before resharing. Misinformation spreads mostly through sharing, not the original post reaching people directly, so the moment that matters is "should I pass this along," not "should I believe this." Checking who posted it and whether it's been reported elsewhere catches most of what matters, without requiring full-time fact-checking.

## AI-generated content needs naming as its own thing

Photos and video that look real but are AI-generated or significantly altered are common enough now that "it looks real" isn't evidence it is. Worth naming specifically, not folding into a general "some stuff is fake" talk — a kid taught to spot old-style photoshop tells won't necessarily catch this.

## What works less well: correcting individual claims

"That's not true, here's the real story" only covers that one claim — the next piece of misinformation needs the same correction from scratch. Teaching the checking habit generalizes; correcting claims doesn't.
