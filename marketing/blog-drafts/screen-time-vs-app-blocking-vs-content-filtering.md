# Screen time limits vs. app blocking vs. content filtering — what's actually different

_Meta description: "Just limit their screen time" isn't one setting — it's three different tools that solve three different problems. Here's what each one actually does, and when you need more than one._

If you've looked at a parental control app's settings screen for the first time, you've probably seen three or four toggles that all sound like they do roughly the same thing: screen time limits, app blocking, content filtering, maybe a bedtime schedule. They're not interchangeable. Each one answers a different question about what's happening on your kid's phone, and most families end up needing at least two of them together.

## Screen time limits: how long

A daily limit answers one question — **how much total time** is your kid spending on their device today. You set a number of minutes, the app tracks usage against it, and once they hit the limit, the device locks (or a specific app locks, depending on how granular the app supports).

This is the right tool when the actual problem is duration — a kid who's fine with *what* they're using, just loses track of *how long*. It does nothing about *which* apps they're using or *what content* they can reach within an allowed app. A kid at their daily limit could have spent the whole time on homework research or the whole time in one game — the limit doesn't distinguish.

**Good for:** general screen-time discipline, "put the phone down" enforcement, kids who genuinely lose track of time rather than deliberately seeking out something specific.

## App blocking: which apps

App blocking answers **which specific apps** are allowed to run at all, independent of how much time is left in the day. You choose per-app: allowed, blocked, or (in more granular setups) allowed only for a certain number of minutes per day.

This is the right tool when the concern is a specific app, not total time — a game that's fine in small doses but shouldn't be unlimited, a social app you want gone entirely for a younger child, or a messaging app that's fine for an older sibling but not the younger one. App blocking is also what actually stops a kid from just switching to a different app once a time limit locks the device — without it, a daily limit only controls the device as a whole, not the specific things happening on it.

**Good for:** managing specific apps by name, different rules per child, stopping "one more game" loopholes that a blanket time limit alone doesn't close.

## Content filtering: what they can reach

Content filtering is different from the first two — it's not about time or specific apps, it's about **categories of content reachable through the browser or any app that loads a web page**. This is usually implemented as DNS-level filtering: certain categories of sites (adult content, gambling, violence, etc.) are blocked at the network level before the page ever loads, regardless of which browser or app is being used to reach it.

This matters because app blocking alone doesn't cover the open web. A child can have every risky *app* blocked and still reach concerning *content* through a completely ordinary browser, unless something is filtering what domains resolve at all. Content filtering closes that gap — and if it's done on-device (resolving DNS locally rather than routing traffic through a third-party cloud filter), it does so without your family's full browsing history passing through an external server.

**Good for:** blocking categories of web content regardless of which app or browser is used to reach them, protecting younger kids from stumbling into something inappropriate rather than deliberately seeking out a specific blocked app.

## Why most families end up using two or three together

None of these three tools substitutes for the other two — they're answering "how long," "which apps," and "what content" as genuinely separate questions. A daily time limit with no app blocking still lets a kid spend the whole allotted time in one game. App blocking with no content filtering still leaves the open web reachable. Content filtering with no time limit still allows unlimited use of whatever's left unblocked.

In practice, most parents start with whichever problem is loudest right now — usually total screen time — and add the others once a new, more specific problem shows up. That's a reasonable way to phase it in; you don't need all three configured perfectly on day one.
