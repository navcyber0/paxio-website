A kid is on a train or in a mall, and a preview pops up on their screen: an image from a device they have never heard of, with a name like "iPhone" or "Galaxy S24". They did nothing. Nobody has their number, their username or their email. Someone in the same room just sent it.

This is sometimes called cyberflashing, and it's a phone problem rather than an internet one, because it depends on physical closeness. Most online safety advice assumes a stranger needs an account to reach your child. This route skips that step.

## How does it work?

Phones have built-in ways to send files to people nearby: AirDrop on iPhones, Quick Share on Android. They use Bluetooth and Wi-Fi to find devices in range, no account or contact details needed. The sender sees a list of nearby device names and picks one, and the receiver usually sees a prompt, sometimes with a preview of the image.

Whether a stranger can see your child's phone at all depends on a visibility setting. On Android, Quick Share lets you choose who can send to you, such as your own devices, contacts only, or everyone for a short window. Settings names and defaults change between phone makers and software versions, so check the menu on your child's actual phone rather than trusting a guide, including this one. On an iPhone, the equivalent AirDrop setting is worth checking in the same way.

## Why this is hard for a kid

The shock of seeing an explicit image is one thing. The harder part is that it happens in public, with no obvious person to blame, and kids often feel embarrassed instead of angry. Many don't mention it. They aren't hiding something; they just don't know whether it's "their fault" or whether telling a parent will lead to losing the phone.

Telling a child in advance that this can happen, and that it is never their fault, does more than any setting.

## What actually helps

1. **Check the receive setting together.** Set it to contacts only or off, and treat "everyone" as a temporary choice for sending a photo to a friend, then switching back. It takes a minute and is worth doing on the phone itself.
2. **Decline, don't open.** A prompt from an unknown device can simply be declined. Opening a preview "just to see" is what the sender wants.
3. **Say it out loud, once.** Something like: "If anyone ever sends you something gross or scary, you did nothing wrong, and you won't lose your phone for telling me." The promise matters, because fear of losing the phone is the main reason kids stay quiet.
4. **Keep the evidence calmly.** If an image arrives, a screenshot of the prompt and device name is useful if you decide to report it to the venue, school or police. In some places, including England and Wales, sending such images is now a criminal offence, but rules differ elsewhere.
5. **Don't blame the phone.** The feature has ordinary uses. The point is controlling who can reach your kid, not removing sharing.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a stranger really send my child an image without knowing their number?

Yes, if the phone's nearby-sharing visibility is open to people who aren't contacts. The sender only needs to be physically close. Setting visibility to contacts only, or turning receiving off, closes that route.

### Does it only happen on iPhones?

No. AirDrop gets most of the press, but Android phones have Quick Share and other nearby-sharing options. The settings differ by phone and software version, so check the specific phone.

### What should my child do if it happens?

Decline the request, don't open the preview, and tell a parent or another trusted adult. A screenshot of the prompt helps if you report it. Reassure them it's not their fault and they won't be punished.
