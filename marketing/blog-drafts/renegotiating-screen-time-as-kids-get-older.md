Somewhere around eleven or twelve, "because I said so" stops working. Your child says, "Everyone in my class gets two hours," or "You never ask me, you just decide," and the strange part is that the second one is often fair.

The rules that suited a nine-year-old were set for a nine-year-old. Keeping them frozen isn't consistency, it's a rule that has quietly stopped matching the person it applies to. But dropping them overnight isn't the answer either.

## Why the old rules start to grate

Between about ten and thirteen, three things change at once. Friendships move onto the phone, so a limit now cuts into the main place plans get made. Schoolwork shifts onto screens. And kids start wanting a say in decisions about their own time, which is a healthy thing to want.

A limit that ignores all three reads, to them, as a verdict on their trustworthiness. That's usually when the arguing gets louder, not because they're being difficult but because the rule's reasons haven't been updated.

## What to keep fixed, and what to loosen

Not everything should be up for debate. A rough split that many families land on:

- **Keep firm:** phones out of the bedroom overnight, no screens at meals, and the apps or content you've decided are off-limits for now.
- **Loosen first:** the daily number, especially on days with homework or a friend's birthday, and how much say they have in *when* the time gets used.
- **Hand over gradually:** small, visible decisions, such as choosing which two nights they'd like extra time. Each one that goes well is evidence for the next.

## Having the conversation

1. **Pick a calm moment,** not the minute after a limit locks.
2. **Ask before you propose.** "What feels unfair about the current rules?" gets you information you can't guess.
3. **Trade, don't concede.** "More time on weekends, and the phone charges outside your room" is a deal. "Fine, whatever" is a retreat.
4. **Agree on a review date,** say a month out. Rules that come with a check-in feel provisional, and that makes them easier to accept.
5. **Write it down in two lines.** It saves a lot of "you said" later.

## Frequently asked questions

### At what age should I loosen screen time limits?

There isn't a fixed age. Many families revisit the rules around 11 to 13 as friendships and schoolwork move onto phones. Look for signs the current rule no longer fits, such as regular arguments, or limits that clash with homework, and review it then.

### Should my child have a say in their own screen time rules?

Yes, within boundaries you set. Kids tend to follow rules they helped shape more readily than ones handed down. You still decide the non-negotiables, like sleep and off-limits content.

### What if "everyone else has more time" is true?

It might be. Ask what specifically they're missing: a group chat, a game with friends, or just the number. You can often solve the actual need without matching the number.
