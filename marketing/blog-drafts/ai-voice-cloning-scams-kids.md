The call comes from an unknown number. It's your kid's voice, upset and talking fast: they're in trouble, they need money now, please don't tell anyone. Most parents would believe it. That's the point. The voice may not be your kid at all. It can be generated from a short clip of them talking, and the tools that do it are cheap and widely available.

## How does an AI voice-cloning scam actually work?

The scammer needs two things: a sample of someone's voice and a person who loves them. The sample can come from a public video, a gaming stream, a voice note, or a few seconds of a kid answering a call from an unknown number. Software turns that into a voice that can say anything the scammer types.

The script is the old "family emergency" scam: an accident, an arrest, a lost phone, a kidnapping. What's new is that it now sounds like the right person. The FBI describes criminals generating "short audio clips containing a loved one's voice" to fake a crisis and ask for money or a ransom ([IC3 public service announcement](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA241203)).

## How common is it?

For the first time, the FBI's annual internet crime report, published in April 2026, has a section on artificial intelligence. In 2025, the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center received 22,364 complaints involving AI, with reported losses of more than $893 million. That covers every AI-assisted scam, not just voice calls. The report singles out "distress" scams, where cloned voices imitate a loved one in trouble: victims reported more than $5 million in losses to those alone, and the FBI notes the scam is spreading to imitations of other family members and friends ([FBI IC3 2025 Annual Report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf), p. 39).

Those are only the cases people reported. Many families who got the call and hung up in time never file anything.

## Why this is a phone problem, not just an internet one

A cloned voice works best on a phone call. The call is live, so there's no time to think. The audio is slightly compressed, so small glitches sound like a bad line. Caller ID proves nothing, because numbers can be spoofed, and the script often explains an unfamiliar number anyway ("my phone died, I'm using a friend's").

Kids can be targets too, not just the source of the voice. A younger child can get a call that sounds like a parent asking them to read out the code that just arrived by text. That code is often the key to an account. ## What actually protects a family

1. **Agree on a family code word.** Pick something that can't be guessed from social media, and agree that anyone calling in a real emergency will say it. This is the FBI's own first recommendation. Practise it once, so a panicking kid remembers it.
2. **Hang up and call back.** Call your kid on the number you already have, or call someone who is with them. A real emergency survives a two-minute callback. A scam usually doesn't.
3. **Treat urgency and secrecy as the warning sign.** "Don't tell Mum," "send it now," and gift cards or crypto are the scam's signature, whatever the voice sounds like.
4. **Shrink the public voice sample.** The FBI also suggests keeping accounts private and limiting followers to people you know. For a kid who posts video, a private profile makes their voice much harder to collect.
5. **Teach kids the reverse rule.** Nobody in the family, including you, will ever ask them for a text code over the phone.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can AI really copy my child's voice from a short clip?

Yes. The FBI describes scammers using short audio clips of a loved one's voice to fake an emergency call. Public videos, voice notes and livestreams are the usual sources, which is why keeping a child's accounts private helps.

### How can I tell if a call is a voice-cloning scam?

Often you can't, just by listening. Instead, ask for your family code word, then hang up and call your child back on the number you already have. Pressure to pay now, keep it secret, or use gift cards or crypto is a strong warning sign.

### What should I do if I already sent money?

Contact your bank or payment provider straight away to try to stop the transfer, then report it. In the US, file a report at ic3.gov. Elsewhere, use your national cybercrime reporting service, such as cybercrime.gov.in in India.
