Sunday evening, the handover. Your child comes back from the other house and says, quite reasonably, "At Dad's I can have my phone until ten." Now you're not arguing about screen time. You're arguing about the other parent, through your kid, and nobody wins that one.

Separated families don't have a screen-time problem that's different in kind. They have the same one, with a second set of rules running in parallel and a child who notices every gap between them.

## What actually needs to match

Most things don't. Kids cope fine with "at Mum's we eat at the table, at Dad's we eat on the sofa." Different homes have different routines, and that's normal. Where a mismatch genuinely causes trouble is a short list:

- **Bedtime and phones at night.** Sleep doesn't reset when the house changes. A child who's up until midnight on one parent's nights arrives at the other's tired, and the week starts badly for everyone.
- **What's off-limits.** If an app or a type of content is banned in one home and freely available in the other, the ban stops meaning much in either.
- **How a limit gets extended.** If one parent says yes to "just ten more minutes" every time and the other never does, the child learns to save the asking for the parent who gives in.

The daily number itself (two hours here, two and a half there) matters less than people expect. Agreeing on those three is usually enough.

## Why "your house, your rules" breaks down with phones

That approach works for most household rules because the rules stay in the house. A phone doesn't. It travels in the school bag, and whatever the last parent set up comes with it. If one parent removed an app limit on a Friday, it's still removed on Monday at the other house, often without anyone mentioning it. Then the next argument is about who changed what, not about the child.

## Agreeing on it without a fight

1. **Talk about the child, not each other's parenting.** "She's been tired at school on Mondays" lands better than "you let her stay up too late."
2. **Write down the short list.** Bedtime for phones, off-limits apps, how extra time is decided. A shared note beats a remembered conversation.
3. **Let the rest differ, on purpose.** Saying out loud "your house can be looser about weekend films" removes a lot of the point-scoring.
4. **Tell your child the same thing together, if you can.** A rule presented by both parents is much harder to play off against itself.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should screen time rules be the same in both homes?

Not all of them. The ones worth matching are phone bedtime, which apps or content are off-limits, and how extra time is decided. The exact daily number can differ a little without causing much trouble.

### What if my co-parent won't agree on screen time rules?

Start with the one rule that affects your time most, usually sleep, and frame it around what you're seeing in your child. If you can't agree, keep your own rules consistent and calm. A predictable home still helps, even if the other one is different.

### How do I stop my child playing one parent off against the other?

Agree in advance how extra time gets decided, and tell your child the same answer from both of you. When the rule doesn't depend on which parent they ask, there's less reason to shop around.
